## 1. The man from Bengbu (0:40–1:55)

[SCREEN] Map of China, zoom into Anhui Province, pins on "Bengbu" and "Hefei"

The story starts in Bengbu, a mid-sized city in Anhui Province, a few hours from Shanghai.

[AI-10] Silhouette of a young man in a dark suit seen strictly from behind, standing at a glass wall above a Chinese city at night, face never visible

There, a businessman named Ding Ning built a group of companies called Yucheng.
It started in manufacturing. Then, like many ambitious firms in China in the early 2010s, it turned to finance.

[SCREEN] Timeline graphic: "2013–2015: China's P2P lending boom" with a counter climbing "→ 3,769 platforms set up (cumulative, Nov 2015, industry data)"

Those were the years of the peer-to-peer lending boom.
The idea sounded modern. An app or a website connects people with savings to businesses that need loans.
No bank in the middle. Higher returns for savers. Faster money for borrowers.
For ordinary savers, the timing was perfect.
Bank deposits paid little. The stock market had just crashed in the summer of 2015. People wanted somewhere safe that still paid something.
By late 2015, according to industry counts, well over three thousand of these platforms had been set up.
And regulation had barely started.

[SCREEN] Timeline: "Feb 2014 — Yucheng buys a Beijing web-finance company / July 2014 — 'Ezubao' goes live"

According to Xinhua, in February 2014 Yucheng bought an internet finance company in Beijing.
In July 2014, it relaunched its platform under a new name: Ezubao.
In Chinese, the name means something like "e-leasing treasure."
That word, leasing, was the whole pitch.

## 2. The leasing machine (1:55–4:37)

[SCREEN] Flow diagram, "The pitch", built step by step: "Investor → Ezubao platform → Yucheng leasing company → Real business leases equipment → Rent flows back → Investor gets 9–14.6%"

Here's how Ezubao said it worked.
A real company needs equipment. Machinery, vehicles, production lines.
Yucheng's leasing company buys that equipment and leases it to the company.
To fund the deal, Ezubao sells pieces of that lease to ordinary investors online.
The company pays rent. The rent flows back. And the investor gets a return.

[SCREEN] Product cards (self-made): "e租年享 / 年安丰裕 … 9%–14.6% a year" vs bank deposit bar "1-yr benchmark 3% → 1.5% (2014–15)"

The court later found that these products, with names like "Annual Enjoyment," promised annual returns of nine to 14.6 percent.
A one-year bank deposit in China paid far less. The official benchmark rate fell from three percent to one and a half.
And according to Chinese prosecutors, you could start with just one yuan, about fifteen cents, and take your money out whenever you wanted.

(beat)

[SCREEN] The same flow diagram; the "Real business" box cracks and turns grey, label "FAKE", and the arrows reroute: "New investors' money → Old investors' 'returns'" + "→ Yucheng's own companies"

Now here's how it actually worked.

[AI-03] A hand sliding a thick folder of company documents across a desk to another hand holding a cash envelope, no faces, no readable text

According to Xinhua's investigation, Ezubao paid companies and middlemen for real companies' information. Their registration papers. Their licenses.
The head of risk control at Yucheng's leasing arm told Xinhua that, on Ding Ning's instructions, they paid one and a half to two percent of the "loan" amount for each company's details.
His department then typed those details into ready-made contracts and posted them online as investment projects.
To make them look stronger, he said, they even changed the companies' registered capital.

[SCREEN] Key-phrase card: "'As far as I know, 95% of the projects on Ezubao were fake.' — Yucheng risk-control head, to Xinhua"

His summary was blunt. "As far as I know, 95 percent of the projects on Ezubao were fake."
Ding Ning himself told Xinhua that the group spent more than 800 million yuan buying this material.

[SCREEN] Icon grid: 207 company icons, 206 fade to grey, one stays lit: "207 'borrowers' checked → 1 real deal"

Police said that of the 207 leasing customers they had checked, only one had done real business with Yucheng.
Some companies only found out when police told them.
One business owner told Xinhua that his firm had once taken a bank loan through a Yucheng guarantee company. Then, in late 2015, his company's bank account was frozen, and police told him Ezubao had listed his company online to raise money in its name.

[SCREEN] Diagram: "Guarantor? Yucheng-controlled (3 guarantee firms + 1 factoring firm)" — every arrow loops back to "Yucheng"

And who guaranteed these loans?
According to Xinhua, Yucheng itself. It controlled three guarantee companies and a factoring company that backed Ezubao's own projects.
Police said the investors' money went into a pool the group controlled.
Xinhua summed up the trick in three phrases: fake projects, fake third parties, fake guarantees.

[SCREEN] Text card: "July 2015 rule: platforms must only match lenders and borrowers — no money pools, no guarantees"

Xinhua pointed out that, under guidelines China's central bank and other regulators issued in July 2015, a lending platform was supposed to be only a matchmaker. No pool of money of its own. No guarantees.
Ezubao was doing both.

## 3. On state TV, on the bullet train (4:37–6:19)

[AI-04] A living room at night lit only by a television, a family's silhouettes on a sofa seen from behind, generic bright advertisement glow on screen, no logos

So why did hundreds of thousands of people trust it?
Part of the answer is advertising. A lot of it.

[SCREEN] Self-made montage card, items appearing one by one: "National TV ✓ / Regional TV ✓ / Subway ✓ / Airports ✓ / Train stations ✓ / A bullet train named after it ✓"

According to the court's judgment, Ezubao advertised on major TV channels across China, in subway stations, airports and railway stations.
It even had a high-speed train named after it: the "Ezubao" bullet train.
Chinese media reported that its ads ran on China Central Television, the state broadcaster, in prime time before the main evening news.

[SCREEN] Count-up: "¥480,000,000+ on advertising (judgment)" ≈ "$70M+"

According to the judgment, it spent more than 480 million yuan on advertising. About seventy million dollars.

[AI-05] A brightly lit forum stage, a speaker silhouette at a podium, rows of audience from behind, generic finance graphics, no logos

And it had a friendly expert.
A financial commentator who appeared in Chinese media joined the company and, according to Chinese reporting on the judgment, spoke for Ezubao at forums and in interviews, vouching for it as an industry expert.
He was later convicted too, and sentenced to four and a half years.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Key-phrase card: "If it's on state TV, it must be safe."

Think about what that does.
In China, state television isn't just another channel. For many families, it's the voice of authority.
If a company is on CCTV, on the bullet train, in the airport, surely someone has checked it.
After the collapse, investors reportedly protested outside CCTV's headquarters in Beijing, saying those ads had helped persuade them to invest.

[AI-06] Sales office at night: rows of empty desks with POS card terminals and stacks of glossy brochures, no people

Ezubao also sold offline.
According to Xinhua, the group ran eight operating centers, including Beijing and Shanghai, and a nationwide network of sales offices.
Victims, the agency reported, were spread across 31 provinces and regions. In practice, most of China.

## 4. Where the money went (6:19–7:26)

[SCREEN] Bar chart, bars growing one by one (court audit, billion yuan): "Paid back to earlier investors 38.4 / Buying indebted companies & bad debts 3.2 / Yunnan partner projects 3.0 / Sent abroad 2.3 / Staff pay & commissions 2.0 / Fees to middlemen for fake projects 1.23 / 'Gifts' to executives 1.2 / Offices 1.2 / Property, planes, cars 0.92 / Luxury goods 0.49 / Advertising 0.48"

So where did the money go?
The court's findings, based on a forensic audit, answer that. And the answer is the definition of a Ponzi scheme.

The biggest share, about 38 billion yuan, went straight back out as principal and interest to earlier investors.
New money, paying old money.

[AI-07] Slow pan across a dark luxury showroom: watches on velvet, a diamond ring under a spotlight, no people

The rest of the list reads like a lifestyle.
About 1.2 billion yuan given to senior managers as "gifts."
About 490 million yuan on luxury goods.
About 920 million yuan on property, aircraft and vehicles.
And around 2.3 billion yuan moved abroad as so-called investments.

[SCREEN] Court card: "Zhang Min, president — received assets worth ~¥700M from Ding Ning (court)" + small text "Xinhua: incl. a villa in Singapore and a pink diamond ring"

The court found that Ding Ning gave the group's president, Zhang Min, assets worth around 700 million yuan.
According to Xinhua, those gifts included a villa in Singapore and a pink diamond ring.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Split card: "Investors were told: equipment leases" vs "The audit found: earlier investors, executives, luxury"

The investors thought they were funding factories and machinery.
They were funding a carousel, and the people at the top were riding it.
And a carousel only keeps turning while new riders keep paying to get on.

## 5. Six days in December (7:26–9:07)

[SCREEN] Timeline graphic building left to right: "Dec 3 → Dec 5 → Dec 8, 2015 → Jan 14, 2016 → Jan 31, 2016"

Like every Ponzi scheme, Ezubao needed more money coming in than going out. By late 2015, that was getting harder.

On December 3rd, 2015, according to Chinese media, police took more than forty people from the platform's Shenzhen branch for questioning.
According to Xinhua, police later found that by December 5th, the group's available cash was running dangerously low.
They also said Yucheng had started moving money and destroying evidence, and that several executives appeared ready to run.

[AI-08] Pre-dawn street in a Chinese city, a convoy of unmarked cars with headlights on, figures from behind entering an office tower lobby, no faces, no insignia

On December 8th, China's Ministry of Public Security ordered a coordinated operation. Police across the country moved on Ding Ning and the group's top executives.
That same day, Xinhua announced that Ezubao was under investigation. The platform went dark.

[AI-09] Crowd of people seen from behind outside a glass office tower in winter, coats and scarves, holding blank placards, no readable text, no faces

For investors, the shock was total.
Within days, according to foreign and Hong Kong reports, investors were gathering outside company offices in several provinces, and protesting in Beijing.

[SCREEN] Text card: "Jan 14, 2016 — 21 people formally arrested (Beijing prosecutors)"

On January 14th, 2016, Beijing prosecutors approved the formal arrest of twenty-one people, including Ding Ning.

[SCREEN] Key-phrase card, slow zoom: "'Ezubao was a complete Ponzi scheme.' — Zhang Min, president, to Xinhua"

Just over two weeks later, Xinhua published its investigation.
In a detention center, Zhang Min, the group's president, told the agency, "Ezubao was a complete Ponzi scheme."
She said senior management all knew it, and that she was deeply sorry.

[AI-02 variant] The excavator pit at dawn, empty, mud and rain, a single sack left on the edge

And that's when the country learned about the pit.
Police told Xinhua the case was enormously complex. More than two hundred servers to search.
And the paper records? More than twelve hundred volumes, in more than eighty sacks, six meters underground, outside Hefei.
Two excavators. More than twenty hours.

## 6. The verdict (9:07–10:25)

[AI-11] Empty courtroom, rows of wooden benches, a shaft of light across an empty defendant's dock, no people, no emblems

In December 2016, Beijing prosecutors filed charges against two Yucheng companies and twenty-six people.

[SCREEN] Ruling card: "Beijing No. 1 Intermediate People's Court — Sept 12, 2017"

On September 12th, 2017, the Beijing No. 1 Intermediate People's Court delivered its verdict.

[SCREEN] Count-up, three numbers in sequence: "¥76.2 billion raised (gross) → ¥59.8 billion (excluding reinvestment) → ¥38 billion+ lost" + "1.15 million+ people"

The court found that from June 2014 to December 2015, through Ezubao and a sister platform called Sesame Finance, the group had used fake leasing and loan projects to take in more than 76 billion yuan.
Strip out money that investors rolled over and reinvested, and it was about 60 billion yuan, roughly nine billion dollars, from more than 1.15 million people.
More than 38 billion yuan, nearly six billion dollars, was lost.

[SCREEN] Gavel icon + text: "Ding Ning — life imprisonment + ¥100M fine (fundraising fraud, smuggling precious metals, illegal possession of firearms, illegally crossing the border)"

Ding Ning was convicted of fundraising fraud, smuggling precious metals, illegally possessing guns, and illegally crossing the border.
He was sentenced to life in prison and fined 100 million yuan.

[SCREEN] Second card: "Ding Dian (brother) — life + ¥70M fine / Zhang Min + 23 others — 3 to 15 years"

His younger brother, Ding Dian, also a senior executive, got life for fundraising fraud.
Zhang Min and twenty-three others were sentenced to between three and fifteen years.

[SCREEN] Third card: "Yucheng International — ¥1.803B fine / Anhui Yucheng — ¥100M fine" ≈ "$290M in total"

The two Yucheng companies were fined a combined 1.9 billion yuan, about 290 million dollars.

In November 2017, the Beijing High People's Court rejected the appeals. The sentences were final.

## 7. Forty cents on the yuan (10:25–11:58)

[AI-12] A small rural kitchen at dusk, an elderly person's hands holding a smartphone showing a blank grey app screen, no face, no readable text

But for the investors, the verdict didn't answer the only question that mattered to them.
How much of my money comes back?

[SCREEN] Self-made "seized assets" board: "Frozen funds ¥10.9B + $80M / Cash ¥918M / 136.9 kg of gold / 1,076 jade, art & jewelry items / Real estate, vehicles, equity" (court, as reported)

According to the judgment, as reported by Chinese media, authorities had frozen almost 11 billion yuan in bank accounts, and seized cash, property, vehicles, nearly 137 kilos of gold, and more than a thousand pieces of jade, art and jewelry.

[SCREEN] Timeline: "July–Aug 2019 — victims register / Jan 16, 2020 — 1st payout (~35%, reported) / Apr 2025 — 2nd payout (cumulative ~40%, reported)"

Then came the waiting.
In 2019, the Beijing court asked victims nationwide to verify and register their losses.
In January 2020, more than four years after the platform went dark, the first payments went out.
The court didn't publish the ratio, but according to Chinese media and victims, it was about 35 percent of the money they had lost.

The second payout only came in April 2025.
Again, the court gave no percentage. Victims told Chinese reporters it added about five percent, bringing the total to roughly forty.
This time, former Ezubao employees were included in the payout too.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Big number card: "~40 cents back for every yuan lost — after almost 10 years" (reported)

[AI-12 variant] The same kitchen at night, the phone face-down on the table, a small stack of bank receipts beside it

One investor told Chinese reporters, quoted by the tech site KrASIA, that after more than four years he had almost forgotten the money existed.
Others had not forgotten for a single day. According to foreign media reports at the time, many of the victims were small investors, including families in rural areas, who had put in their savings.

Forty cents on the yuan. After almost ten years.
For people who were promised fourteen percent a year.

## 8. The day the P2P industry hit zero (11:58–12:48)

[SCREEN] Line chart falling off a cliff: "P2P platforms in China — peak ~5,000 → 3 (Nov 6, 2020) → 0 (mid-Nov 2020)" (CBIRC)

Ezubao wasn't the only one. It was the warning nobody fully heeded.
Over the next few years, China's P2P lending boom became a wave of collapses, defaults and runaway founders.
Regulators tightened the rules, then shut the sector down.

In November 2020, China's banking regulator said the number of P2P platforms had fallen from a peak of about five thousand to zero.
Three months earlier, its chairman had said lenders were still owed more than 800 billion yuan by failed platforms. Around 115 billion dollars.

[AI-13] Aerial night shot of a dense Chinese city, thousands of apartment windows, lights going out one by one

An entire industry, gone in about five years.
At its peak, according to Caixin, it had around fifty million investors.
Many of them are still waiting for their money.

## 9. The pattern (12:48–13:34)

[SCREEN] Five-step checklist card, each line ticking on: "1. High returns, called 'safe' / 2. A real-sounding business you can't check / 3. Your money back 'any time' / 4. Credibility borrowed from TV, trains, experts / 5. A guarantor that is really the same company"

Strip away the app and the bullet train, and the shape is familiar.
One. High returns, sold as safe.
Two. A real-sounding business you can't check. A lease on a machine you'll never see.
Three. A promise you can take your money out any time.
Four. Credibility borrowed from television, trains and experts.
And five. A guarantee that, when you follow the arrows, leads back to the same company.

(beat)

None of this is unique to China. The same shape has turned up in Korea, in Vietnam, across Asia and around the world.
That's not investment advice. It's a pattern. And a pattern is harder to bury than paper.
