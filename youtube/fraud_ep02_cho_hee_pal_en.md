## 1. The machine that paid every day (0:48–3:12)

[SCREEN] Map of South Korea, zoom into Daegu in the southeast. Caption: "Daegu — one of Korea's largest cities"

The story begins in Daegu, a big industrial city in South Korea's southeast.
Daegu is known as a conservative, tight-knit place. Neighbors know neighbors. Word of mouth matters.
That would turn out to be important.

[AI-10] Silhouette of a middle-aged man in a suit seen from behind, standing at a window overlooking a city at dusk, face never visible

The man at the center of this story was Cho Hee-pal, born in 1957.
Korean media, citing investigators, described him as someone with only an elementary-school education, who reportedly worked as a laborer and drifted through small businesses, and who learned the multi-level marketing trade in his forties.
In October 2004, he set up a company in Daegu called BMC.
And it started selling a deal that sounded almost too simple.

[AI-03] Rows of empty massage chairs and health devices in a dim bathhouse lounge, steam in the air, no people

Korea is famous for its public bathhouses and saunas, called jjimjilbang.
The pitch went like this.
You buy a health device. A massage machine, a pelvic-correction bed, even a karaoke machine. One unit cost 4.4 million won, roughly four thousand dollars at the time.
You never take it home. The company rents your machine out to bathhouses and clinics.
And you get paid every day.

[SCREEN] Self-made infographic walkthrough, step 1: "You pay ₩4,400,000 = 1 machine"

Let's walk through it the way an investor would have seen it.
Step one. You pay 4.4 million won. That's one machine.

[SCREEN] Step 2: a bank-app counter adding "+₩35,000" every business day

Step two. According to Korean reporting from the time, about 35,000 won lands in your account almost every business day. That's around thirty dollars, every day.

[SCREEN] Step 3: "166 payments ≈ 8 months → ₩5,810,000 back"

Step three. After about 166 payments, roughly eight months, you've received about 5.8 million won.
Your money back, plus 1.4 million won on top.

[SCREEN] Bar chart: "Advertised: 35% a year (court) / The math: ~48% annualized / Bank deposit: ~5%"

The court that later tried the group's number two described the promise as a fixed 35 percent a year.
Run the numbers on eight months, and it works out closer to 48 percent annualized.
A normal Korean bank deposit at the time paid around five percent.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Text card: "유사수신 (yusa-susin) = taking deposits without a license"

Korea actually has a word, and a whole law, for this kind of business.
It's called yusa-susin. Roughly: unlicensed deposit-taking.
It means collecting money from the public and promising fixed returns, without being a bank, and without any license to do it.
It's illegal in Korea for one simple reason. It is the classic shape of a Ponzi scheme.

## 2. The ladder (3:12–5:02)

[SCREEN] Self-made pyramid infographic: ranks stacking upward, "부장 → 국장 → 본부장 → 단장 → 사업전무"

The daily payment was the hook. The ladder was the engine.
According to a December 2008 report by the Korean news magazine Sisa IN, investors were organized into ranks, like a company. Department head. Bureau chief. Division head. Group leader. Executive director.

[SCREEN] Two branching lines, each filling with 12 machine icons: "24 machines ≈ ₩100M → salary + bonuses"

To become a department head, the report said, you had to run two lines of investors below you, and each line had to bring in twelve machines.
Twenty-four machines. Around 100 million won of other people's money.
Only then did the company pay you a salary and bonuses.

[SCREEN] The two lines explode to 140 icons each: "Next rank: 280 machines"

To climb to the next rank, the requirement reportedly jumped to 280 machines.
Think about what that does to a person.
You are no longer just an investor. You are a recruiter. Your income depends on your sister, your old classmates, the women from your church, the man who runs the shop downstairs.

[AI-05] Crowd of faceless people seen from behind filling a seminar hall, a bright stage ahead

And the system had a second trick.
According to the same report, the operation kept changing its name. BMC. Then a series of new company names, one after another, with different brands for different regions.
Prosecutors later found that the group ran around twenty companies in total.
When one name drew attention, the money simply moved to the next one.

[SCREEN] Simple diagram: "New investors' money → paid out as 'rental income' to old investors"

But there was a basic problem underneath all of it.
Machines rented to saunas could never earn that kind of money.
According to prosecutors, the "rental income" being paid out was largely new investors' money, recycled to earlier ones.
When the medical device story wore thin, the pitch reportedly shifted to real estate and redevelopment projects.
The product changed. The promise didn't.

## 3. From Daegu to everywhere (5:02–6:47)

[AI-04] Glowing dots spreading across a dark map of Korea like a network, city by city

The money did arrive. At first.
Early investors were paid on time. They told friends. They told family. And they earned commissions for bringing them in.

[SCREEN] Animated map: Daegu → Gyeongbuk → Busan/Gyeongnam → Chungcheong → Seoul/Gyeonggi/Incheon, with "50+ centers" counter

From Daegu, the network spread down to Busan and the southern coast, up through the Chungcheong provinces, and into Seoul, Gyeonggi and Incheon.
By late 2008, Korean media were counting around fifty sales centers across the country.

[AI-11] Women's hands, seen from above, around a café table covered with brochures and a calculator, no faces

So who signed up?
Not bankers. Not professional investors.
In the Seoul area, the same 2008 report said, about seven in ten investors were homemakers in their forties and fifties. Later reporting described large numbers of retirees, people who had lost jobs, and families still recovering from the Asian financial crisis of the late 1990s.
Some reportedly put in their retirement money. Some borrowed to invest more.

(beat)

Why them?
Think about where a middle-aged homemaker or a new retiree stood in Korea in the mid-2000s.
Interest rates were low. Pensions were thin. Many had a lump sum, a severance payment or a matured insurance policy, and nowhere obvious to put it.
And the person making the pitch was rarely a stranger. It was a friend from the neighborhood, or a relative, someone you had trusted for twenty years.

You put in your money. And then, every single day, a payment lands in your account. Like clockwork.
After a few weeks, the doubts fade. After a few months, you're the one telling your sister she's missing out.
That daily payment wasn't just a return. It was proof. Or it looked like proof.

## 4. The warnings (6:47–8:08)

[SCREEN] Timeline graphic building left to right: "2006 → May 2007 → Aug 2007 → Apr 2008 → Sep 2008 → Oct 2008"

Here's what makes this story hard to watch.
The scheme did not hide in the dark for four years. The warnings are on the record.

According to police and prosecutors, police began looking into the group as early as 2006.
In May 2007, a police station in Miryang, in South Gyeongsang Province, opened a formal investigation into companies linked to Cho.

[AI-12] A thick paper file lying on a dark desk under a desk lamp, a hand slowly closing it, no face

And then something happened that a court would later describe in detail.
In August 2007, Kang Tae-yong, Cho's right-hand man, handed fifty million won, twice, to a police sergeant at the Daegu police agency. A sergeant who was working on the Cho case.
A district court later found that the sergeant, in return, dragged out the investigation and gave the group "investigative convenience."
Prosecutors also alleged that, when police asked for transaction records, the group handed over records from an old, defunct company instead of the one actually taking the money.

[SCREEN] Self-made document card (no real logos): "April 2008 — financial regulator alerts police in Daegu, Busan, Incheon"

In April 2008, according to Sisa IN, Korea's financial regulator, the Financial Supervisory Service, flagged two of the group's companies for illegal deposit-taking, and notified police in Daegu, Busan and Incheon.
In September 2008, victims began filing complaints.
By then, it was too late.

## 5. The collapse and the escape (8:08–10:08)

[SCREEN] Timeline graphic: "Oct 2008 — offices raided / wanted list / Dec 2008 — gone"

In the autumn of 2008, the global financial crisis was shaking markets everywhere.
In Korea, investigators were finally closing in.
In October 2008 the payments stopped, and the companies collapsed.
According to Korean reporting at the time, the group's central computer systems were wiped and the accounts were emptied before police got there.

[AI-01 variant] The same harbor at night, a coastguard light sweeping across empty water

Cho was put on the wanted list.
Some victims reportedly photocopied the wanted posters themselves and carried them to ports around the country.
But according to investigators, on December 9th, 2008, he boarded a fishing boat from a small port in Taean, on the west coast.
At sea, he reportedly switched to another vessel and crossed to China.

His right-hand man, Kang Tae-yong, had already fled to China the month before.
Kang was the group's administrative vice president. He ran the money and the computer systems.

[SCREEN] Big number, counting up: "₩5,071,500,000,000"

Years later, prosecutors and the courts put a number on what the group had collected.
About 5 trillion won. Roughly 4 billion dollars.
From around 70,000 people.

[SCREEN] Split card: "Money that flowed in: ~5 trillion won" vs "Actual losses (prosecutors' estimate): ~840 billion won"

That 5 trillion figure counts money that went round and round, as people reinvested their "profits."
Prosecutors estimated the real, unrecovered losses at about 840 billion won. Around 700 million dollars.
And they found that the group had stashed away about 290 billion won.
Korean media started calling it the biggest fraud since the founding of the nation.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Text card: "Remember one document. We'll come back to it." (retention hook, ~9:40; pays off at 10:08 and 13:30)

Before we go on, keep two things in mind.
One: a cheque, handed over in a hotel coffee shop, to a man whose job was to catch Cho.
Two: a single piece of paper from China, dated ten days before the death it was supposed to record.
The first explains how he got away. The second is why so many people still don't believe he's gone.

## 6. The twist: who was on the payroll? (10:08–12:29)

[AI-06] A gloved hand sliding a thick envelope across a dark desk under a green banker's lamp

Here's the part that turned a fraud case into a national scandal.
How did one man run a scheme this big, for this long, and then simply sail away?

(beat)

[SCREEN] Self-made "court ruling" card (no real logos): "Former police sergeant (case investigator) — 8 years (Daegu District Court, Sept 2016)"

We've already met the first one. The police sergeant on the case.
The court found he took 200 million won from Kang in total. Two payments in 2007. And a third, on October 31st, 2008, at a hotel in Daegu, while the group's offices were being searched.
A district court sentenced him to eight years in prison.

[AI-13] A hotel coffee-shop table at night, two cups, a folded cheque slid under a saucer, only hands visible

[SCREEN] Second ruling card: "Former senior police officer — 9 years in prison (Supreme Court, final, Dec 2016)"

The second was more senior.
In October 2008, he headed the violent-crime unit at the Daegu regional police agency.
According to the courts, on October 30th, 2008, he met Cho in person, in the coffee shop of a hotel in Daegu, and received 900 million won as a bank cheque. That's around 800 thousand dollars.
The courts found that he took the money in return for investigation information. According to reporting on the ruling, he sat face to face with a man his own agency was hunting, and let him walk away.
He was sentenced to ten years at first, reduced to nine on appeal. The Supreme Court confirmed it in December 2016.

[SCREEN] Third ruling card: "Former prosecution official — 9 years + ₩1.4B fine (Supreme Court, final, Oct 2016)"

It went further.
An official at the Daegu prosecutors' office, who had worked on the Cho case itself, was convicted of taking bribes over five years, from 2008 to 2013.
Most of it, the court found, came from a businessman who was managing the group's hidden money.
In return, the official passed on prosecution information and helped bury investigations.
His sentence: nine years in prison and a 1.4 billion won fine, confirmed by the Supreme Court in October 2016.


[AI-07] Long empty corridor of a government building at night, one office door slightly open, light spilling out

By the time the investigation was done, prosecutors said eight police and prosecution officials had faced punishment.
For the victims, this changed everything.
The people who were supposed to catch him, some of them, had been paid by him.

## 7. The funeral nobody believed (12:29–15:58)

[SCREEN] Map animation: Taean → across the Yellow Sea → Shandong Province, China

So where was Cho?
According to investigators, he moved around China, reportedly living under another name.
Then, in May 2012, Korean police made an announcement.

[SCREEN] Self-made police-announcement card: "May 21, 2012 — Police: Cho died of a heart attack in China, Dec 2011"

Cho Hee-pal, they said, had died in China in December 2011, of an acute heart attack.
Police said they had found Chinese emergency-care records and a death certificate while searching the homes of his family and associates.

[AI-08] A funeral altar in a dim room, white chrysanthemums, an empty picture frame, mourners seen only from behind

They pointed to a video of his funeral, reportedly filmed by family members.
According to Korean reporting at the time, he had been cremated in China within days, and his ashes brought back to Korea before the end of December.
And Korean authorities only found out about it five months later.

(beat)

The public didn't buy it.
Cremation meant there was no body to test.
Even the police kept him on the wanted list, saying the death had not been confirmed one hundred percent.

[SCREEN] Self-made document card: "Cremation certificate — issued: Dec 11 / date of death: Dec 21" (as reported by victims' groups, 2015)

Remember that piece of paper?
In 2015, victims' groups pointed to a Chinese cremation document that, according to Korean reporting, was issued ten days before the date of death it recorded.
They also noted that early reports and later official accounts named different Chinese cities as the place where he died.
A man who said he was a former aide reportedly told Korean media he had spoken with Cho on the phone after the date of his supposed death.
There were rumors of plastic surgery, of a new name, of sightings in China and even back in Korea.

[SCREEN] Text card: "These are the public's doubts. They were never proven."

None of those rumors were ever proven. But the questions were understandable.
After all, this was a case where officials had already been caught taking money.
In 2015, Korean TV investigative programs took the story up again, and public pressure grew.

[AI-14] Night street in a Chinese city, rain on the pavement, two plain-clothes figures seen from behind walking toward an apartment entrance

Then, on October 10th, 2015, Chinese police arrested Kang Tae-yong, the right-hand man, in the city of Wuxi.
On December 16th, he was flown back to Korea.
Arriving at the prosecutors' office in Daegu, he was asked by reporters if Cho was alive.
He said he had seen Cho dead with his own eyes.

[SCREEN] Self-made prosecution-announcement card: "June 28, 2016 — Prosecutors: Cho died in December 2011"

In June 2016, after a two-year reinvestigation, prosecutors announced their conclusion.
They said Cho collapsed after a night of drinking at a hotel in Weihai, in China's Shandong Province, and died at a hospital in the early hours of December 19th, 2011.
They said they had consistent testimony from people who were with him, and from fourteen people at the funeral.
They said a DNA test on hair matched Cho, and that the funeral video showed no signs of editing.
They said they had found no evidence that he was alive.

Because he was dead, they said, he could not be charged. The case against him was closed.
Some victims' groups rejected that conclusion and said they would keep searching.
They argued that a strand of hair proves who it came from, not whether that person is still alive.

(beat)

So, is Cho Hee-pal dead?
The official answer from Korean authorities is yes.
The doubts have never been proven. But for many victims, they have never gone away either.

## 8. The trial of the right-hand man (15:58–17:18)

[AI-15] Empty courtroom at dusk, rows of wooden benches, a single shaft of light across the defendant's chair, no people, no emblems

With Cho officially dead, Kang Tae-yong became the highest-ranking member of the group ever to stand trial.
Prosecutors asked for life in prison.

[SCREEN] Ruling card: "Daegu District Court, Jan 13, 2017 — 22 years + ₩12.5B forfeiture"

In January 2017, a Daegu court found that, from June 2006 to October 2008, together with Cho, he had collected around 5 trillion won from roughly 70,000 people through unlicensed deposit-taking, promising a fixed 35 percent a year.
It convicted him of fraud, embezzlement, bribery, and hiding criminal proceeds.
The court also found that he had paid the police sergeant on the case for investigation information.
And it found that, in China, he had worked with organized-crime figures to try to collect money he believed he was owed.
The judges called it a property crime almost without precedent, one that had destroyed families.
He was sentenced to 22 years.

On appeal, his lawyers argued the sentence was too harsh, and that some of the charges were really Cho's doing.
The appeal court kept the 22 years. In November 2017, the Supreme Court confirmed it.

[SCREEN] Gavel icon + text: "Kang Tae-yong — 22 years in prison, 12.5 billion won forfeiture (Supreme Court, Nov 2017)"

Prosecutors charged around seventy people in total over the scheme.
But the man whose name was on the whole thing would never face a judge.

## 9. The villain on the big screen (17:18–18:04)

[AI-16] A dark cinema auditorium seen from the back row, silhouettes of an audience lit by the glow of the screen, no image visible on screen

That gap, a crime with no trial for the man at the top, is part of why the story escaped the courtroom and moved into pop culture.
In December 2016, the Korean crime film "Master" opened in theaters. It's about a smooth-talking chairman who runs a giant investment fraud, and tries to fake his way out of the country.
Its director said the villain was modeled on Cho Hee-pal.
More than seven million people saw it in Korean cinemas.

[SCREEN] Text card: "단군 이래 최대 사기 — 'the biggest fraud since the founding of the nation'"

And the phrase Korean media had used, "the biggest fraud since the founding of the nation," stuck.
For a whole generation, Cho's name became shorthand for a con man who never paid.

## 10. The money, 18 years later (18:04–19:40)

[AI-02 variant] The same dim apartment, now morning light, an envelope from a court lying unopened on the table

But what about the victims?
Prosecutors said about 95 billion won had been tracked down and recovered.
Against the losses, that's a small fraction.
Spread across 70,000 people, it's roughly 1.4 million won each. About a thousand dollars.

[SCREEN] Self-made timeline: "Dec 2017 — first payout / 2018–2025 — court fights / June 2026 — payouts restart"

And getting even that has taken years.
About 71 billion won of the criminal proceeds was deposited with a court in Daegu, split into four separate cases, to be divided among creditors.
The first payout came in December 2017: 32 billion won, to more than 17,000 creditors.
Then some victims challenged who should count as a creditor, and how much each should get. Everything froze.
Those lawsuits only ended in October 2025.

[SCREEN] Big number card: "June 24, 2026 — ₩32B to 22,156 creditors"

On June 24th, 2026, the court in Daegu restarted the payouts: another 32 billion won, to more than 22,000 creditors, with money due to go out from July.
Two smaller pots, of 5 billion and 2 billion won, are still waiting for dates.
Korean media reported that more than 20,000 victims were represented by only around a hundred appointed representatives, and that many had moved, changed numbers, or simply lost contact. The court said it had to track people down through local administrative offices.
Some, after eighteen years, will never see the money at all.

(beat)

[AI-09] An old woman's hands, seen from above, folding a faded investment certificate into a drawer (no face)

For many families, the damage was never just financial.
Korean media reported that some victims lost homes, marriages, and their health.
Many have spent nearly two decades waiting.

## 11. Why it keeps coming back (19:40–20:55)

[AI-17] A smartphone glowing on a dark table, a chat bubble with a rising green chart, a hand hovering over it, no face, no real app logos

Here's the uncomfortable part.
Cho's scheme collapsed in 2008. The idea behind it never did.

[SCREEN] Self-made chart: "Unlicensed deposit-taking reports to Korea's financial regulator — 2023: 328 / 2024: 410" + "35 companies referred to police (2024)"

In February 2025, Korea's financial regulator said it had received 410 reports about unlicensed deposit-taking in 2024, up a quarter from the year before. It referred 35 companies to police.
The packaging has changed. Instead of massage chairs, it's new technology, crypto assets, real estate.
But look at the shape.

[SCREEN] Five-step checklist card, each line ticking on: "1. A fixed, guaranteed return / 2. Paid often, so it feels real / 3. A product story you can't check / 4. Bonuses for bringing in others / 5. Time — and someone who looks away"

One. A fixed, guaranteed return, far above what any bank pays.
Two. Payments that come often, daily or weekly, so the scheme feels real long before it can be checked.
Three. A product story you can't easily verify. A machine in a sauna you'll never visit.
Four. Bonuses for bringing in others, so victims become recruiters.
And five. Time. Every month that nobody stops it, the hole gets deeper, and the people inside it get more people involved.

(beat)

None of this is unique to Korea. Versions of the same shape have turned up across Asia and around the world.
That's not investment advice. It's a pattern. And patterns are the one thing a Ponzi scheme can't hide.

## 12. Outro (20:55–21:47)

[AI-01 variant] The harbor at dawn, empty, the water calm

Officially, the story ended in a hotel room in China in 2011.
For many of the people who lost everything, it never ended at all.

(beat)

The scheme didn't run on machines. It ran on trust.
Trust in a daily payment. Trust in the friend who brought you in.
And trust that if something was wrong, the authorities would stop it.

So here's the question to ask, every time: if a business pays you more than any bank ever could, where is that money really coming from?

Next time: another country, another promise that sounded safe. And another fortune that simply disappeared.

Subscribe so you don't miss it.
