# EP.02 The Funeral Nobody Believed: Korea's $4 Billion Ponzi Scheme

- Channel: Paper Tiger Files (fraud & financial crime stories, Asia focus)
- Target length: ~12 min (~1,750 words of narration at ~145 wpm)
- Tone: true-crime storytelling, calm, tense, never mocking victims
- Notation: `[SCREEN]` = editing cue, `[AI-xx]` = AI video shot (prompts at the bottom), `(beat)` = 1-second pause
- Legal rule (Korea): only court rulings and official police/prosecution announcements are stated as fact. Everything else is "reportedly" / "according to police" / "prosecutors alleged". Cho Hee-pal himself was **never tried** (the case against him was closed because of his reported death), so his role is described through the convictions of his associates and the prosecutors' findings. No victims or private individuals are named. Convicted officials are described by rank only, never by name.
- Spelling: "Cho Hee-pal" (조희팔), "Kang Tae-yong" (강태용) throughout. Money: give won first, then a rounded dollar figure.

## 1. The machine that paid every day (0:45–2:45)

[SCREEN] Map of South Korea, zoom into Daegu in the southeast. Caption: "Daegu — one of Korea's largest cities"

The story begins in Daegu, a big industrial city in South Korea's southeast.
Daegu is known as a conservative, tight-knit place. Neighbors know neighbors. Word of mouth matters.
That would turn out to be important.

[AI-10] Silhouette of a middle-aged man in a suit seen from behind, standing at a window overlooking a city at dusk, face never visible

The man at the center of this story was Cho Hee-pal.
Korean media have described him as someone from a modest background, with no banking or finance training, who reportedly drifted through small businesses before this one.
In late 2004, a company linked to him in Daegu started selling a deal that sounded almost too simple.

[AI-03] Rows of empty massage chairs and health devices in a dim bathhouse lounge, steam in the air, no people

Korea is famous for its public bathhouses and saunas, called jjimjilbang.
The pitch went like this.
You buy a medical or massage device for about 4.4 million won, roughly four thousand dollars at the time.
The company rents your machine out to bathhouses and clinics.
And you get paid every day.

[SCREEN] Self-made infographic: "Invest 4.4M won → 8 months → get back 5.81M won" with a counter ticking up daily

According to Korean reporting on the case, the promise was about 5.8 million won back within eight months.
That works out to returns of around 48 percent a year.
At the time, a normal Korean bank deposit paid around four or five percent.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Text card: "유사수신 (yusa-susin) = taking deposits without a license"

Korea actually has a word, and a whole law, for this kind of business.
It's called yusa-susin. Roughly: unlicensed deposit-taking.
It means collecting money from the public and promising fixed returns, without being a bank, and without any license to do it.
It's illegal in Korea for one simple reason. It is the classic shape of a Ponzi scheme.

## 2. Growing like a pyramid (2:45–4:30)

[AI-04] Glowing dots spreading across a dark map of Korea like a network, city by city

The money did arrive. At first.
Early investors were paid on time. They told friends. They told family. They brought in new investors, and earned commissions for it.

The company and its sister firms spread from Daegu to Incheon, Busan, and beyond.
Prosecutors later found that the group ran around twenty different companies.

[AI-05] Crowd of faceless people seen from behind filling a seminar hall, a bright stage ahead

Many of the investors were not wealthy.
Korean media reported that a lot of them were retirees, people who had lost jobs, and families still recovering from the Asian financial crisis of the late 1990s.
Some reportedly put in their retirement money. Some borrowed to invest more.

Think about how it felt from the inside.
You put in your money. And then, every single day, a payment lands in your account. Like clockwork.
After a few weeks, the doubts fade. After a few months, you're the one telling your sister she's missing out.
That daily payment wasn't just a return. It was proof. Or it looked like proof.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Simple diagram: "New investors' money → paid out as 'rental income' to old investors"

But there was a basic problem.
Machines rented to saunas could never earn 48 percent a year.
According to prosecutors, the "rental income" being paid out was largely new investors' money, recycled to earlier ones.
When the medical device story wore thin, the pitch reportedly shifted to real estate and redevelopment projects.
The product changed. The promise didn't.

## 3. The collapse and the escape (4:30–6:00)

[SCREEN] Timeline graphic: "Oct 2008 — offices raided / Nov 2008 — wanted / Dec 2008 — gone"

In the autumn of 2008, the global financial crisis was shaking markets everywhere.
In Korea, investigators were closing in.
In October 2008 the payments stopped, and the companies collapsed.

[AI-01 variant] The same harbor at night, a coastguard light sweeping across empty water

Cho was put on the wanted list.
But according to investigators, on December 9th, 2008, he boarded a fishing boat from a small port in Taean, on the west coast.
At sea, he reportedly switched to another vessel and crossed to China.

His right-hand man, Kang Tae-yong, had already fled to China the month before.
Kang ran the group's money and its computer systems.

[SCREEN] Big number, counting up: "₩5,071,500,000,000"

Years later, prosecutors put a number on what the group had collected.
About 5 trillion won. Roughly 4 billion dollars.
From around 70,000 people.

[SCREEN] Split card: "Money that flowed in: ~5 trillion won" vs "Actual losses (prosecutors' estimate): ~840 billion won"

That 5 trillion figure counts money that went round and round, as people reinvested their "profits."
Prosecutors estimated the real, unrecovered losses at about 840 billion won. Around 700 million dollars.
And they found that the group had stashed away about 290 billion won.
Korean media started calling it the biggest fraud since the founding of the nation.

## 4. The twist: who was on the payroll? (6:00–7:30)

[AI-06] A gloved hand sliding a thick envelope across a dark desk under a green banker's lamp

Here's the part that turned a fraud case into a national scandal.
How did one man run a scheme this big, for this long, and then simply sail away?

(beat)

[SCREEN] Self-made "court ruling" card (no real logos): "Former senior police officer — 9 years in prison (Supreme Court, final)"

Years later, Korean courts gave part of the answer.
A senior police officer who, in October 2008, headed a violent-crime unit at a regional police agency, was convicted of taking 900 million won from Cho.
That's around 800 thousand dollars.
The court found he took it in return for investigation information, while Cho was a fugitive.
He was sentenced to nine years in prison.

[SCREEN] Second ruling card: "Former prosecution investigator — 9 years in prison (final)"

It went further.
An official at the Daegu prosecutors' office, who had worked on the Cho case itself, was convicted of taking bribes from Cho's associates, and of turning a blind eye while the group's money was laundered.
His nine-year sentence was confirmed by the Supreme Court in 2016.

And a senior prosecutor in Seoul was convicted in a separate bribery case that, according to court reporting, included money from Cho's circle.

[AI-07] Long empty corridor of a government building at night, one office door slightly open, light spilling out

By the time the investigation was done, prosecutors said eight police and prosecution officials had faced punishment.
For the victims, this changed everything.
The people who were supposed to catch him, some of them, had been paid by him.

## 5. The funeral nobody believed (7:30–9:15)

[SCREEN] Map animation: Taean → across the Yellow Sea → Shandong Province, China

In May 2012, Korean police made an announcement.
Cho Hee-pal, they said, had died in China in December 2011, of an acute heart attack.

[AI-08] A funeral altar in a dim room, white chrysanthemums, an empty picture frame, mourners seen only from behind

Police pointed to a death certificate issued by Chinese authorities, to Chinese medical records, and to a video of his funeral, reportedly filmed by family members.
He had been cremated. His ashes were reportedly brought back to Korea.

(beat)

The public didn't buy it.
Cremation meant there was no body to test.
A man who said he was a former aide reportedly told Korean media he had spoken with Cho on the phone after the date of his supposed death.
There were rumors of plastic surgery, of a new name, of a quiet life somewhere in China.
Victims asked simple questions. Why was the death announced only months later? Why was the body cremated so quickly? Could paperwork from another country, filed by people close to him, really settle the matter?
None of those rumors were ever proven. But the questions were understandable.
After all, this was a case where officials had already been caught taking money.
Korean TV investigative programs dug into the story again and again.
And the police kept him on the wanted list.

[SCREEN] Text card: "These are the public's doubts. They were never proven."

Then, in October 2015, Chinese police arrested Kang Tae-yong, the right-hand man, in an apartment in Wuxi.
Two months later he was sent back to Korea.
Korean media reported that Kang told investigators he had seen Cho's death for himself.

[SCREEN] Self-made prosecution-announcement card: "June 28, 2016 — Prosecutors: Cho died in December 2011"

In June 2016, after a two-year reinvestigation, prosecutors announced their conclusion.
They said Cho collapsed after a night of drinking at a hotel in Weihai, in China's Shandong Province, and died at a hospital in the early hours of December 19th, 2011.
They said they had consistent testimony from his companion and from fourteen people at the funeral.
They said a DNA test on hair matched Cho, and that the funeral video showed no signs of editing.

Because he was dead, they said, he could not be charged. The case against him was closed.
Some victims' groups rejected that conclusion and said they would keep searching.
They argued that a strand of hair proves who it came from, not whether that person is still alive.

(beat)

So, is Cho Hee-pal dead?
The official answer from Korean authorities is yes.
The doubts have never been proven. But for many victims, they have never gone away either.
The case even crossed into pop culture. The 2016 Korean crime film "Master," about a fraudster who fakes his way out of the country, was inspired in part by the Cho case, according to its director.

## 6. The money, 18 years later (9:15–11:00)

[SCREEN] Gavel icon + text: "Kang Tae-yong — 22 years in prison, 12.5 billion won forfeiture (Supreme Court, Nov 2017)"

Kang Tae-yong went on trial.
The courts found that, together with Cho, he had collected around 5 trillion won from roughly 70,000 people through unlicensed deposit-taking.
In November 2017, Korea's Supreme Court confirmed his sentence: 22 years in prison.
Prosecutors charged around seventy people in total over the scheme.

[AI-02 variant] The same dim apartment, now morning light, an envelope from a court lying unopened on the table

But what about the victims?
Prosecutors said about 95 billion won had been tracked down and recovered.
Against the losses, that's a small fraction.
Spread across 70,000 people, it's roughly 1.4 million won each. About a thousand dollars.

[SCREEN] Self-made timeline: "Dec 2017 — first payout / 2018–2025 — court fights / June 2026 — second payout"

And getting even that has taken years.
About 71 billion won of the criminal proceeds was deposited with a court in Daegu, to be divided among creditors.
The first payout came in December 2017.
Then disputes over who should get what, and how much, went to court, and the process froze.
Those lawsuits only ended in October 2025.
In June 2026, the court started the second payout: 32 billion won, to more than 22,000 creditors.
Korean media reported that many victims had moved, changed numbers, or lost contact with the representatives handling their claims.
Some, after eighteen years, will never see the money at all.

(beat)

[AI-09] An old woman's hands, seen from above, folding a faded investment certificate into a drawer (no face)

For many families, the damage was never just financial.
Korean media reported that some victims lost homes, marriages, and their health.
Many have spent nearly two decades waiting.

## 7. Outro (11:00–12:00)

[AI-01 variant] The harbor at dawn, empty, the water calm

Officially, the story ended in a hotel room in China in 2011.
For many of the people who lost everything, it never ended at all.

(beat)

The scheme didn't run on machines. It ran on trust.
Trust in a daily payment. Trust in the friend who brought you in.
And trust that if something was wrong, the authorities would stop it.

So here's the question to ask, every time: if a business pays you more than any bank ever could, where is that money really coming from?

[SCREEN] Teaser card: a dark map of Asia, one red dot pulsing

Next time: another country, another promise that sounded safe. And another fortune that simply disappeared.

Subscribe so you don't miss it.
And tell me in the comments: have you ever been offered a deal that paid every single day?
