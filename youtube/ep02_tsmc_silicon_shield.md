## 1. Starting over at 56 (0:45–3:00)

[SCREEN] 1980s American office (stock) + caption "1983"

This story begins with a man who got passed over.
His name was Morris Chang.
Born in China in 1931, he spent his childhood moving from city to city to escape war.
He came to America as a young man, studied engineering, and joined a brand-new industry: semiconductors.

He spent more than 25 years at Texas Instruments, one of America's chip pioneers.
He rose to the top ranks, running its semiconductor business.
He was known for a bold idea there.
Price your chips low from day one, before they're cheap to make.
Win the volume, and the costs will fall as your factory learns.
Remember that idea. It will matter later.

But in 1983, the CEO job went to someone else.
So he left.
He took a top job at another company. It didn't last a year.
He was in his fifties. To most people, his career looked like it was winding down.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Hsinchu, Taiwan (stock) + caption "1985: Moving to Taiwan"

Then Taiwan called.
Back then, Taiwan was known for cheap assembly work. Toys, shoes, simple electronics.
The government wanted more. It wanted high tech.
It picked semiconductors as the way up. And it picked Morris Chang to lead the charge.
In 1985, he became head of Taiwan's top government research institute.

[SCREEN] Title card "February 1987: TSMC is born" + caption "Founder's age: 56"

And in February 1987, at the age of 56, he started a company.
Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company. TSMC.

[SCREEN] Pie chart: "Taiwan government fund ~48% / Philips ~28% / Others"

Nearly half the money came from the Taiwanese government.
The manufacturing technology and licenses came from Philips, the Dutch electronics giant.
From day one, this was a company raised by a nation.

## 2. A business nobody wanted (3:00–5:00)

[SCREEN] Comparison graphic: left "Design + Manufacture", right "Manufacture only"

Here's the thing. TSMC's business model went against everything the industry believed.
In the 1980s, chip companies did it all.
They designed their own chips, built them in their own factories, and sold them.
Intel did it. The Japanese giants from our last episode did it.

Morris Chang had a different idea.
"We won't design chips. We'll build the chips other people design."
That's called a foundry. Contract manufacturing for chips.
TSMC was the world's first company built to do only that.

[SCREEN] Empty order book + ticking clock effect

At first, it was a hard sell.
Who would trust their most important product to an unknown company in Taiwan?
In the early years, TSMC survived on orders the big players didn't want to bother with.
Leftover work. Older technology. Small batches.
It wasn't glamorous. But every order taught TSMC how to run a factory better than the last one.
That was the Texas Instruments lesson again. More volume, more learning, lower costs.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Small startup office (stock) + caption "Chip companies with no factory"

But the idea opened a huge door.
Building a chip factory costs a fortune.
You could have a brilliant chip design and still have no way to make it.
TSMC gave those companies a way in.
Soon, a whole new kind of company appeared. Chip companies that only design, and own no factories at all.
The industry even has a name for them: "fabless," meaning no fab, no factory.
Today, some of the most valuable chip companies in the world work exactly this way.
They draw the blueprints. Someone else builds the chips.
More often than not, that someone is TSMC.

[SCREEN] Big text: "NEVER COMPETE WITH YOUR CUSTOMERS"

TSMC had one golden rule.
Never compete with your customers.
TSMC didn't sell chips under its own name. So it had no reason to steal anyone's designs.
For customers, that meant one thing. You could hand over your deepest secrets.
That trust became TSMC's most valuable asset.

## 3. Two fateful partnerships (5:00–7:00)

[SCREEN] 1990s graphics card + caption "1997: A letter"

In the late 1990s, the head of a small graphics chip company wrote Morris Chang a letter.
His complaint? TSMC's sales team wasn't taking his company seriously.
On a trip to the U.S., Chang picked up the phone and called him himself.

The man's name was Jensen Huang.
The company was Nvidia.

[SCREEN] Nvidia logo + TSMC logo, handshake animation

Back then, Nvidia was tiny. A few dozen employees.
It made graphics chips, the chips that draw images for video games.
Its survival was riding on a single chip.
In 1998, Nvidia signed a deal making TSMC its main manufacturing partner.
A small customer. But Chang didn't treat it like one.
Nobody could have guessed what that partnership would become 25 years later.

(beat)

[SCREEN] 2014 smartphone launch imagery + caption "A8 chip"

The second partnership was Apple.
Apple had been getting its iPhone chips made by another company.
Then in 2014, with the iPhone 6, it started moving production to TSMC.
By 2016, with the iPhone 7, TSMC was making all of them.

[SCREEN] iPhones stacking up by the hundreds of millions

Think about what that means.
Huge volumes of the newest chips. Every year. On a fixed date. No room for error.
Apple's orders were money. They were also brutal training.
TSMC poured the profits into the next generation of factories.
And step by step, it pulled ahead of everyone else.
When you make chips for the pickiest customer on Earth,
every other customer sees that as the ultimate stamp of approval.

## 4. The factory becomes a shield (7:00–8:50)

[SCREEN] Text graphic styled like a book cover (not the real cover): "Silicon Shield, 2001"

In 2001, a journalist published a book.
It was called *Silicon Shield*.
The argument went like this.

[SCREEN] Map: Taiwan and mainland China with the strait highlighted + caption "~23 million people"

First, some context for anyone outside Asia.
Taiwan is an island of about 23 million people, sitting off the southeast coast of China.
It has its own government, its own military, its own elections.
But Beijing considers Taiwan part of China, and has never ruled out using force to take it.
That tension has hung over the strait for more than seventy years.

So here's the book's idea.

The world depends on Taiwan's chips.
So if China attacked Taiwan, America would suffer. The global economy would suffer.
Even China itself would suffer.
So semiconductors become an invisible shield protecting Taiwan.

[SCREEN] Map of China + caption "One of the world's biggest chip buyers"

That last part matters most. China gets hurt too.
China is one of the biggest buyers of chips on the planet.
A lot of those chips are tied to Taiwan.
If Taiwan's factories stop, China's factories could stop with them.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Supply-chain arrows spreading from Taiwan to the U.S., Europe, Japan, and China

Over the years, that idea became more and more real.
In 2025, TSMC held roughly 70 percent of the global foundry market.
For the most advanced chips, estimates go above 90 percent.

In Taiwan, people even have a nickname for TSMC.
"The sacred mountain that protects the nation."

But not everyone buys it.
Some say a shield that valuable can also become a prize worth taking.
A shield can cut both ways.

[SCREEN] Screen-flip effect, music cuts out abruptly

And here's the twist.
Which country grew the most nervous about this shield?
It wasn't China.
It was Taiwan's most important friend. The United States.

## 5. Move the shield (8:50–10:40)

[SCREEN] U.S. Capitol → construction site in the Arizona desert

Look at it from Washington's point of view.
Nearly all of the most important chips come from one island,
across the ocean, in a region where tensions keep rising.
If those factories stop, American tech stops. Maybe American defense too.
Remember our last episode? America treats dependence on critical chips as a national security problem.

[SCREEN] World map with three pins: Arizona, USA / Kumamoto, Japan / Dresden, Germany

So TSMC started going abroad.
In 2020, it announced a factory in Arizona.
By late 2024, that factory was mass-producing advanced chips.
TSMC's planned U.S. investment has since grown to around 165 billion dollars.
It hasn't been smooth. There have been steady reports of worker shortages, and costs far higher than in Taiwan.

In February 2024, a TSMC factory opened in Kumamoto, Japan. The one we mentioned at the end of last episode.
It was built with major support from the Japanese government.
And that August, TSMC broke ground on its first European factory, in Dresden, Germany, together with European partners.
America, Japan, Europe. All of them wanted a piece of the shield on their own soil.

[SCREEN] Event-style graphic + quote caption: "Globalization is almost dead."

Morris Chang stepped down in 2018.
But one thing he said afterward still gets quoted all the time.
At a ceremony at the Arizona factory in December 2022, at 91 years old, he said this:
"Globalization is almost dead. Free trade is almost dead."
In other words, the era of making things wherever it's cheapest and best is over.
Now you make things wherever it's safest.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Shield icon cracks into pieces that scatter across the globe

And this is where Taiwan's dilemma begins.
The shield's power comes from one fact: it only exists in Taiwan.
So the more TSMC spreads its factories around the world,
doesn't the world have a little less reason to protect Taiwan?

That's why TSMC keeps its most advanced technology in Taiwan first.
Build abroad, yes. But keep the heart at home.
Share with the world, but never give it all away. It's a delicate balance.

## 6. King of the AI era, and outro (10:40–12:00)

[SCREEN] AI data center server racks (stock) + caption "2025 revenue: ~$122 billion"

And now, AI is making that shield even thicker.
Nvidia's AI chips. Apple's newest chips. Custom AI chips designed by Big Tech.
Most of them go through TSMC.
And modern AI chips aren't just about making one great chip.
You also need advanced packaging, stitching several chips together into one.
TSMC plays a key role there too.
In 2025, chips for AI accelerators made up a high-teens percentage of TSMC's total revenue. And that share keeps climbing.
The bigger AI gets, the harder it is to imagine AI without TSMC.

In 2025, TSMC's revenue hit about 122 billion dollars. An all-time record.
That summer, its market value passed one trillion dollars.
And by the end of the year, it began mass-producing 2-nanometer chips.
Where? In Taiwan.

(beat)

[SCREEN] The opening Taiwan Strait map returns, this time zooming out

In 1987, a 56-year-old man started over with one factory.
Today, that factory is the world economy's pressure point. And a nation's shield.
But remember this.
The most valuable shield is often the first thing an enemy aims at.
And a shield only works as long as the world still needs what's behind it.

[SCREEN] Next-episode teaser: GPU silhouette + "From gaming chips to AI empire"

There's one company keeping TSMC's factories busier than anyone.
The tiny company that teamed up with TSMC after a single letter.
How did a company that made graphics cards for gamers become the king of the AI era?
Next time: Nvidia.

And tell me in the comments: will the silicon shield keep protecting Taiwan?
