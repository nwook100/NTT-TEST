## 1. From a market stall to an empire (0:42–1:56)

[SCREEN] Map of Vietnam, zoom into Ho Chi Minh City. Caption: "Ho Chi Minh City — Vietnam's financial capital"

Ho Chi Minh City. Vietnam's biggest city and its money capital.
In the 1980s and 90s, as Vietnam opened its economy, fortunes were made here fast.

[AI-03] A busy covered market at dawn, stalls of cosmetics and hair accessories, warm light, shoppers seen from behind, no faces

Truong My Lan was born in Saigon in 1956, into a family of Chinese descent.
According to state media and international reporting, she started out selling cosmetics with her mother at a stall in Ben Thanh Market, the city's oldest market.

[SCREEN] Timeline card: "1956 born · 1992 Van Thinh Phat founded · 2012 SCB formed · 2022 arrest"

In 1992, she founded a company called Van Thinh Phat.
Over the next thirty years, it grew into one of the biggest property groups in the country.
Luxury hotels. Office towers. Shopping malls in the heart of District 1.

[SCREEN] Self-made photo-strip of downtown landmarks (Wikimedia CC photos), labels: "Times Square Saigon · Windsor Plaza Hotel · …", stamp "linked to Van Thinh Phat"

She married a Hong Kong businessman, and kept a low public profile.
To most Vietnamese, she was simply one more name in a booming property market.
Her niece would later run the group as its chief executive. Her husband stood beside her in business. It was, in many ways, a family empire.
But building towers takes enormous amounts of money.
And the cheapest money, if you can get it, is a bank's.

## 2. Buying a bank, quietly (1:56–3:14)

[SCREEN] Three small bank icons merging into one: "Dec 2011: three troubled banks → Saigon Commercial Bank (SCB)"

In late 2011, Vietnam's central bank pushed three struggling lenders to merge into one: Saigon Joint Stock Commercial Bank, known as SCB.
It was supposed to be a rescue.
According to the court, it became something else.

[SCREEN] Compare card: left "On paper: Lan owns ~5%" / right "Court finding: controls more than 91.5%"

Vietnamese law limits how much of a bank any one person can own. On paper, Lan held a small stake. State media put it at about five percent.
But the Ho Chi Minh City court later found that she was the real owner of more than 91.5 percent of SCB's shares.

[AI-04] A conference table in a dim boardroom, a dozen empty leather chairs, name placards with no readable text, one chair at the head pulled back

How do you own nine-tenths of a bank while appearing to own almost nothing?
Proxies.
According to investigators, relatives, employees and associates held shares in their own names. The court said the people holding more than 75 percent of the shares all admitted they were acting for her.

[SCREEN] Org-chart graphic: "Truong My Lan" at top, dotted lines to faceless "proxy shareholder" icons, then to "SCB board & executives"

And with the shares came the people.
The court found that from 2012 she picked the bank's key managers, set up special units to handle lending and payouts, and gave instructions directly.
Officially, she was not on SCB's board.
In practice, the court said, the bank answered to her.

## 3. The piggy bank (3:14–5:17)

[SCREEN] Title card: "HOW THE MONEY LEFT THE BANK" (4 steps)

So how do you move billions of dollars out of a bank you secretly control, without anyone noticing?
The court laid out the method. Let's walk through it.

[SCREEN] Flow diagram, step 1: "Set up companies" → icon grid filling to "1,000+"

Step one. Companies.
According to state media, investigators counted more than a thousand companies in the Van Thinh Phat network. Many of them existed mainly on paper. Ghost companies, with people listed as directors who often didn't run anything.

[SCREEN] Step 2: "Ghost company → loan application → SCB" with a stamp "APPROVED"

Step two. Loans.
Those companies applied to SCB for loans. Fake applications, the court found, approved by managers who knew where the money was going.
Between 2018 and 2022 alone, the court counted 916 such loan applications, worth around 545 trillion dong.

[SCREEN] Step 3: a small apartment icon with a price tag inflating like a balloon: "Collateral value ×3"

Step three. Collateral.
Every loan needs security. According to the court, appraisal firms colluded to inflate the value of the assets pledged, so the paperwork looked covered.

[SCREEN] Step 4: arrows from SCB fanning out to "Van Thinh Phat projects", "old loans", "cash", "abroad"

Step four. The money moves on.
Once disbursed, the loan money was shuffled between accounts, used to pay off older loans, poured into Van Thinh Phat's projects, or taken out in cash.
Each step, on its own, looked like ordinary banking. A loan. A valuation. A transfer. Only together did they form a pipe that ran in one direction: out.

[AI-01 variant] Close-up of a worn notebook on a van dashboard, columns of handwritten numbers, a gloved hand turning the page, no readable names

And here's where the driver comes in.
According to prosecutors, from February 2019 to 2022, Lan's driver carried cash from SCB to her company's headquarters and to her home. His notebook added up to 108 trillion dong, plus 14.7 million US dollars.

[SCREEN] Math card: "Largest note: ₫500,000 → 108 trillion = 216 million notes" (label "our math")

Even in Vietnam's largest banknote, that is more than two hundred million notes.

[SCREEN] Big number stack: "2,500+ loans · 10 years · ₫304 trillion embezzled (≈ $12.5B)"

Over ten years, the court said, SCB made more than 2,500 loans into Lan's network.
It found that she had embezzled 304 trillion dong. About 12.5 billion dollars.
International media noted that was close to three percent of Vietnam's entire economy in 2022.

## 4. Paying the referee (5:17–6:25)

[AI-05] Three plain white foam boxes stacked on the floor of a dim office, tape on the lids, a desk lamp throwing a hard shadow, no people

A hole that big should be visible from the outside.
Banks in Vietnam are inspected by the State Bank, the central bank.
And SCB was inspected.

[SCREEN] Timeline: "SBV inspection of SCB" → red stamp "RESULTS ALTERED" → caption "court finding"

According to the court, the bank's general director paid the head of a central-bank inspection team 5.2 million dollars, in four payments.
International reporting from the trial described some of that cash being delivered in styrofoam boxes.
In return, the court found, the inspection findings were softened. SCB's real condition stayed hidden.
So the bank kept looking healthy on paper. Depositors kept depositing. And the hole kept growing.

[SCREEN] Ruling card: "Former central-bank inspection chief — bribery — LIFE IMPRISONMENT (upheld on appeal, Dec 2024)"

The inspection chief was sentenced to life in prison. Her appeal was rejected.
She was one of fifteen former central-bank officials who stood trial alongside Lan, along with officials from the Government Inspectorate and the State Audit Office.

(beat)

Think about what that means.
The people whose job was to check the bank were, according to the court, part of how the bank stayed unchecked.

## 5. The bonds sold at the counter (6:25–7:54)

[AI-02 variant] A bank branch interior with glossy posters, an older couple seen from behind at a counter, a staff member in uniform smiling, no faces, no logos

But by 2018, the loans weren't enough.
So, the court found, Lan's group found a second source of cash: ordinary savers.

[SCREEN] Flow diagram: "4 companies → 25 bond issues (2018–2020) → sold through a securities firm & SCB branches → 35,824 buyers"

Four companies linked to Van Thinh Phat issued 25 batches of corporate bonds between 2018 and 2020.
The court found these bonds were essentially fake: issued without real security, and the money raised was never used for the stated purpose.
They were sold through a securities firm and SCB's own branches.
To a saver, the difference between a deposit and a bond can look like a few lines of small print. A deposit is a claim on the bank, backed by deposit insurance. A corporate bond is a loan to a company. If the company can't pay, you can lose it.

[SCREEN] Key phrase: "As safe as a deposit?"

According to bondholders interviewed by state media, bank staff told them the bonds were safe. Many said they had walked in to renew a savings account and walked out with bonds.
State media also reported that the bank's general director held training sessions for thousands of sales staff on how to sell them.

[SCREEN] Count-up: "35,824 victims · ₫30 trillion (≈ $1.2B)"

The court counted 35,824 victims and more than 30 trillion dong taken, about 1.2 billion dollars.
Where did it go? Repaying bank loans. Paying interest on older bonds. Projects. Transfers abroad. And, the court said, Lan's personal spending.

## 6. The run (7:54–8:56)

[AI-06] Long queue of people outside a modern bank branch at dawn, seen from behind and from far away, umbrellas, uneasy mood, no logos

In October 2022, it all came apart.
Police arrested Truong My Lan.

[SCREEN] Timeline, Oct 2022: "Lan arrested" → "crowds at SCB branches" → "State Bank places SCB under special control"

Within days, worried depositors crowded SCB branches to pull out their savings.
For many families, SCB was simply the bank down the street. They had no idea who really controlled it.
The State Bank placed SCB under special control and sent in managers from the big state-owned banks.

[SCREEN] Compare card: "₫304T embezzled (court)" vs "~$24–26B in central-bank support to SCB (Reuters)"

To keep the bank alive, the central bank kept lending to it.
By 2025, Reuters reported, nearly 26 billion dollars had been pumped into SCB.
And the bondholders discovered that the companies behind their bonds could not pay.
According to state media, groups of them gathered again and again outside offices in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi, asking for their money back.
In the end, public money was keeping a private bank alive, because letting it fail could have spread panic through the whole system.

## 7. The trial (8:56–10:09)

[SCREEN] Exterior photo of the Ho Chi Minh City People's Court (Wikimedia CC), caption "March 5, 2024"

On March 5, 2024, the trial opened at the Ho Chi Minh City People's Court.
It was part of the Communist Party's long-running anti-corruption campaign, known as the "blazing furnace".

[SCREEN] Number wall, one after another: "86 defendants · ~2,700 witnesses summoned · ~200 lawyers · 104 boxes of evidence, 6 tonnes"

Eighty-six defendants. About 2,700 people summoned as witnesses. Around 200 lawyers. And, according to the BBC, 104 boxes of case files weighing six tonnes.
Lan denied embezzling. She said she had only a small stake in the bank, and blamed her subordinates.
The court rejected that. It said she had orchestrated a sophisticated criminal enterprise.

[AI-07] Empty courtroom at dusk, rows of benches, a single shaft of light across an empty defendant's dock, no emblems, slow push-in

On April 11, 2024, the court gave its verdict.

[SCREEN] Ruling card: "Truong My Lan — embezzlement: DEATH · bribery: 20 yrs · banking violations: 20 yrs" + "Pay SCB ≈ ₫674 trillion (≈ $27B)"

Truong My Lan: guilty of embezzlement, bribery, and violating banking rules.
The sentence: death.
The court ordered her to pay back around 674 trillion dong, about 27 billion dollars, to SCB.
It said there was little chance the money could all be recovered.

[SCREEN] Ruling card row: "Husband — 9 yrs · Niece (Van Thinh Phat CEO) — 17 yrs · 4 defendants — life · others — 3 yrs suspended to 20 yrs"

Her husband got nine years. Her niece, who ran Van Thinh Phat, got seventeen.
Four defendants were sentenced to life: two former chairmen of the bank, its former general director, and the central-bank inspection chief.
Every one of the 86 was found guilty.

## 8. Death row, then a second trial (10:09–11:29)

[SCREEN] Timeline: "Oct 17, 2024 · 2nd trial: LIFE" → "Dec 3, 2024 · death sentence upheld" → "Apr 21, 2025 · bond fraud cut to 20 yrs" → "Jun 2025 · law changed"

In the autumn of 2024, Lan was back in the same court, this time for the bonds.
On October 17, she was found guilty of fraud, money laundering, and illegally moving money across borders.
The court found she had laundered more than 445 trillion dong, and moved about 4.5 billion dollars in and out of Vietnam. She was sentenced to life.

[SCREEN] Key phrase: "Repay ¾ → life instead of death"

In December 2024, an appeals court upheld her death sentence.
But under Vietnamese law, it noted, she could avoid execution if she paid back at least three-quarters of what she had taken.
International media called it a race against time: her life seemed to depend on how fast her empire could be sold.
In April 2025, a second appeals court cut her bond-fraud sentence from life to twenty years, after money began to be recovered for the bondholders.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Text card: "June 2025 — National Assembly removes the death penalty for embezzlement (in force July 1, 2025)"

Then the law itself changed.
In June 2025, Vietnam's National Assembly abolished the death penalty for eight crimes, including embezzlement.
Death sentences already handed down for those crimes would no longer be carried out.
According to international reporting, Truong My Lan's death sentence was commuted to life imprisonment.

## 9. Where's the money? (11:29–12:49)

[AI-08] A warehouse of auction lots under harsh light: a luxury handbag in a glass case, a black limousine under a dust cover, numbered tags, no logos, no people

For the victims, the question was never her sentence. It was their money.

[SCREEN] Self-made bar: "Owed to bondholders: ₫30.08T" with a fill bar rising round by round: "Jun 2025 → Jul 2026: 10 payouts ≈ ₫12.3T (~41%)"

Investigators had frozen more than 1,200 properties linked to her, and hundreds of millions of SCB shares held in her name or her proxies' names.
Ho Chi Minh City's civil judgment enforcement office began paying bondholders in June 2025.
By May 2026, according to the Vietnamese government's news portal, nine rounds had paid out about 12 trillion dong, roughly 40 percent of what was owed, to more than 42,000 creditors.
A tenth payment followed in July 2026. It was worth about one percent of what was owed.
For a retiree who put their savings into a bond they believed was a deposit, that means waiting, year after year, for a slice at a time.

[SCREEN] Auction cards: "2 handbags ≈ $539,000 · Maybach ≈ $630,000 · yacht — no buyer (yet)"

Her possessions are being sold. Two designer handbags. A Maybach. Other cars. A yacht that, at first, nobody wanted to buy.
She had asked to keep the handbags for her family. She was refused.

[SCREEN] Compare card: "Bondholders: ~41% repaid" vs "SCB: ≈ $27B ordered — a fraction recovered"

But the bondholders are only part of the bill.
The 27 billion dollars owed to SCB is on another scale entirely. Prosecutors said much of it may never come back.

## 10. The pattern (12:49–13:53)

[SCREEN] Five-step checklist card, each line ticking on: "1. The lender and the borrower are secretly the same person / 2. Loans hidden behind shell companies / 3. Inflated collateral / 4. Inspectors who look away / 5. Risky products sold as 'safe' to savers"

Strip away the numbers, and the shape is simple.
One. The lender and the borrower are secretly the same person.
Two. The loans hide behind companies that exist mostly on paper.
Three. The collateral is inflated, so everything looks covered.
Four. The people who are supposed to check, don't. Or are paid not to.
And five. When the hole grows, it gets filled with money from ordinary savers, sold something risky as if it were a deposit.

(beat)

In this case, one detail made it worse. The bonds were sold inside the bank, by people wearing the bank's uniform. The trust people had in the bank was the product.
This is not unique to Vietnam. Insider lending has broken banks all over the world.
If someone at a bank counter offers you a product with a better rate than a deposit, ask one question: who is actually borrowing my money?
