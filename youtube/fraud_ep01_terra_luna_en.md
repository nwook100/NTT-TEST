## 1. The golden boy (0:53–2:13)

[AI-03] Stylized silhouette of a young man in a hoodie on a startup stage, spotlight, crowd silhouettes (no real face)

Do Kwon grew up in Seoul, studied computer science at Stanford, and worked briefly as a software engineer at Microsoft and Apple.
Then he came home. In 2016, he started a small wireless networking company in Korea called Anyfi.
It was a modest startup. What came next was not.

[SCREEN] Two dark silhouette cards side by side, names as text only: "DO KWON — the coder" / "DANIEL SHIN — the dealmaker"

In early 2018, Kwon co-founded Terraform Labs with Daniel Shin, one of Korea's best-known startup founders.
Shin had built Ticket Monster, or TMON, one of the country's big e-commerce platforms, and he knew merchants all over Korea.
The pairing made sense. Kwon had the code. Shin had the customers.

[SCREEN] Follower counter ticking up toward "~1,000,000" over a dark silhouette of a phone (no real profile shown)

Kwon also had something else: a voice. On Twitter, he was sharp, funny, and supremely sure of himself.
By 2022, around a million people followed him. To many of them, he wasn't just a founder. He was the future.

[SCREEN] Simple diagram: "Bitcoin = price goes up and down" vs "Stablecoin = always $1"

Terra's big idea was a stablecoin.
Most cryptocurrencies swing wildly in price. A stablecoin is supposed to stay at one dollar. Always.
That stability is what lets people use crypto like real money.

Most stablecoins do this the boring way. For every coin, they hold a real dollar, or something like it, in reserve.
Terra didn't.

## 2. Coffee paid in crypto (2:13–3:28)

[AI-09] Close-up of a hand tapping a smartphone at a small café counter, a payment terminal glowing, steam from a coffee cup, no logos, no faces

But before we look at how Terra worked, you need to see how it was sold.
Because in the beginning, the pitch wasn't about getting rich. It was about buying coffee.

In 2019, Shin's other company launched a mobile payments app in Korea called Chai.
Shoppers got discounts. Merchants paid lower fees than the card companies charged.
And Terra told the world that, behind the scenes, those payments were running on the Terra blockchain.

[SCREEN] Simple diagram: "Customer pays in won" → "KRT (Terra's won-pegged coin)" → "Merchant"

Terra didn't only have a dollar coin. It also had a coin pegged to the Korean won, called KRT.
In 2019, Chai partnered with Terraform to bring that won coin into its app. Every coffee, every online order, was supposed to be a tiny proof that Terra worked as money.

[SCREEN] Quote card, attributed: "Terra said: 500,000 users, 2.7 million transactions"

Terra's own announcements boasted of hundreds of thousands of users and millions of transactions.
For investors, this was the killer argument.
Most crypto projects were just promises. Terra could point to real people, in a real country, paying for real things.

(beat)

Keep the name Chai in mind. It comes back later in this story. In a courtroom.

## 3. The magic trick (3:28–5:00)

[SCREEN] Animated infographic: two coins, UST and LUNA, connected by a two-way arrow labeled "swap $1 worth"

Terra had two coins.
UST, the stablecoin meant to stay at one dollar.
And LUNA, its sister coin, whose price could rise and fall.

The system promised that you could always swap one UST for one dollar's worth of LUNA, and back.
If UST dropped below a dollar, traders could buy it cheap, swap it for a dollar of LUNA, and pocket the difference.
In theory, that buying pressure would push UST back to a dollar. Forever.

[SCREEN] Worked example, step by step: "Buy 1 UST for $0.97" → "Swap for $1.00 of new LUNA" → "Profit: 3¢" → "That UST is burned"

Here's what that looks like.
Say UST slips to 97 cents. You buy one. You hand it to the system, which destroys it, and gives you one dollar's worth of brand-new LUNA. You've made three cents.
Thousands of traders doing that at once remove UST from circulation, until the price climbs back to a dollar.
And if UST goes above a dollar, it works the other way: burn LUNA, create UST, sell it.

(beat)

[AI-04] A tower of glowing coins balanced on a single thin glass pillar, slowly rotating

There was no vault of dollars behind it. Just code, and confidence.
And the whole thing depended on one assumption: that LUNA would always be worth something.

Critics called it what it looked like: a design that could spiral to zero if confidence ever broke.
Kwon's response to critics was famous for its arrogance.
When one of them challenged him online, he replied: "I don't debate the poor on Twitter."

## 4. The 20% promise (5:00–6:37)

[SCREEN] Big number, slowly counting up: "~20% per year"

Then came the bait.
A lending platform built on Terra, called Anchor Protocol, offered close to 20 percent annual interest on UST deposits.
Your bank was offering almost nothing. Anchor offered nearly 20 percent, on a coin that was supposed to be as stable as a dollar.

[AI-05] Crowd of faceless people walking toward a glowing golden door in the dark

Money poured in.
By early 2022, the vast majority of all UST in existence was sitting in Anchor, earning that yield.
LUNA's price soared above 100 dollars. Terra's total value reached tens of billions of dollars.
Terraform even signed a five-year sponsorship deal with the Washington Nationals baseball team, reported at about 38 million dollars.
In Korea, Kwon was hailed as a crypto genius. His fans called themselves "Lunatics."

(beat)

But where was that 20 percent actually coming from?
For a long time, the honest answer was: largely from Terra itself, subsidizing the payments to keep people in.

[SCREEN] Self-made bar: "Anchor yield reserve" draining day by day, then a sudden "+450M UST" refill

Anchor kept a reserve to cover the gap between what borrowers paid and what depositors were promised.
By early 2022, that reserve was draining by more than a million dollars a day.
So in February 2022, the Luna Foundation Guard, an organization we'll meet properly in a minute, approved a top-up of 450 million dollars' worth of UST, just to keep the 20 percent going.
Shortly after, Anchor's community voted to let the rate slowly come down. But by then, the promise had already done its work.

## 5. The hidden rescue (6:37–8:00)

[SCREEN] Timeline flashback graphic: "May 2021 — one year earlier"

Here's the twist most people never heard at the time.
In May 2021, a year before the final collapse, UST had already lost its one-dollar peg.
And then, mysteriously, it recovered.

[AI-06] A shadowy hand reaching from behind a curtain to steady a falling coin

The public story was that the code worked. The algorithm fixed itself.
But years later, U.S. regulators told a different story.
According to the SEC, the recovery was driven by a large trading firm quietly buying UST behind the scenes, under a secret deal with Terraform.

[SCREEN] SEC order on screen, key lines highlighted: "May 23, 2021", "verbal agreement", "more than $20 million UST"

The SEC's account goes like this. On May 23, 2021, as UST slid, Terraform made a verbal agreement with the firm.
Terraform would loosen the restrictions on the firm's right to buy LUNA at a discount.
In return, according to the SEC, the firm bought more than 20 million dollars of UST to push the price back up.
The SEC said that investors were misled into believing the algorithm had saved the peg.
The trading firm's affiliate later settled with the SEC without admitting or denying the claims.

(beat)

If that's true, the system hadn't passed its stress test. It had been rescued. And the rescue was hidden.
So who knew? And would anyone ever admit it? Hold on to that question.

## 6. The bitcoin war chest (8:00–10:05)

[AI-10] A vast dark vault, rows of glowing orange coins stacked on steel shelves, a heavy vault door slowly swinging open, teal rim light

By early 2022, the warnings were getting louder. Kwon's answer was a war chest.
In January 2022, a nonprofit called the Luna Foundation Guard was set up in Singapore.
Its job was to build a reserve that could defend UST's peg in an emergency.
Kwon said the goal was 10 billion dollars' worth of bitcoin.

[SCREEN] Self-made deal card: "Feb 2022 — $1B LUNA sale. Led by: Jump Crypto, Three Arrows Capital"

The first big chunk of money came in February: a one-billion-dollar sale of LUNA to investors, led by two heavyweight crypto firms, Jump Crypto and a hedge fund called Three Arrows Capital.
Remember that second name. It comes back too.

[SCREEN] Counter climbing: "0 → 80,394 BTC" with a small "≈ $2.4B (May 7, 2022)" label

And it bought fast. On some days that spring, it was buying more than 100 million dollars of bitcoin at a time.
Kwon said he expected to reach the 10-billion-dollar target by the end of the third quarter.
Anyone could watch the reserve wallets fill up on the blockchain, in real time.
By early May, it held 80,394 bitcoin, worth around 2.4 billion dollars. It had become one of the biggest bitcoin holders in the world.
To many investors, this looked like the missing piece. The vault Terra never had, finally being filled.

(beat)

But there was a catch.
The reserve was a fraction of the nearly 19 billion dollars of UST in circulation.
And it was held in bitcoin, an asset that could fall at exactly the moment it was needed.
U.S. prosecutors later said there was another problem: Kwon presented the foundation as independent from Terraform, when, according to prosecutors, he controlled it.

[SCREEN] RETENTION HOOK — two question cards appear one by one: "1. Was the 2021 rescue hidden?" / "2. Did Chai really run on Terra?"

So before we watch the fall, here are two questions to carry with you.
Was the 2021 rescue really hidden from investors? And were those Chai payments really running on Terra?
Both get answered before the end of this video. One by a jury. The other by Do Kwon himself.

## 7. Seven days in May (10:05–12:38)

[AI-11] Calendar pages tearing off one by one in a dark room, each page glowing red at the edges, ticking clock sound

[SCREEN] Day stamp: "SATURDAY, MAY 7, 2022"

Saturday, May 7, 2022.
Anchor is holding about 14 billion dollars' worth of UST.
Terraform pulls a large amount of UST out of a key trading pool on a platform called Curve, as part of a planned move to a new pool.
Within minutes, other wallets dump tens of millions of UST into what's left.
The pool tilts. UST slips to 99 cents.
It sounds tiny. It isn't. For a coin whose whole promise is one dollar, a penny is a crack in the glass.

[SCREEN] Day stamp: "SUNDAY, MAY 8"

Sunday. Anchor depositors head for the exit. Billions of dollars of UST leave in a matter of days.
The Luna Foundation Guard says it will lend 1.5 billion dollars, half in bitcoin and half in UST, to defend the peg.
Kwon tweets: "Deploying more capital. Steady lads."

[SCREEN] Day stamp: "MONDAY, MAY 9"

[AI-07] Drone shot over a city at dawn, digital red rain falling between skyscrapers

Monday. The defense isn't working.
The foundation's bitcoin starts leaving its wallets to be sold. Bitcoin itself slides toward 30,000 dollars.
Anchor deposits fall from 14 billion to under 9 billion. UST, at one point, drops to around 35 cents.

[SCREEN] Animated death-spiral diagram: UST falls → more LUNA printed → LUNA price falls → less trust → UST falls

And now the magic trick runs in reverse.
To defend the peg, the system creates more and more LUNA.
More LUNA means each LUNA is worth less. Which means less trust in UST. Which means more selling.

[SCREEN] Day stamp: "TUESDAY–WEDNESDAY, MAY 10–11"

Tuesday and Wednesday. Exchanges choke on the flood of transactions. The world's largest exchange, Binance, briefly pauses LUNA and UST withdrawals.
LUNA, worth around 80 dollars at the start of the month, falls below one dollar.

[SCREEN] LUNA supply counter exploding from hundreds of millions into the trillions

The supply of LUNA explodes from hundreds of millions of coins into the trillions.

[SCREEN] Day stamp: "THURSDAY–FRIDAY, MAY 12–13"

Thursday. The Terra blockchain itself is halted. By Friday, it has been halted again.
Major exchanges pull LUNA and UST from trading. LUNA is now worth a tiny fraction of a cent.
That Friday, Kwon writes: "I am heartbroken about the pain my invention has brought on all of you."

[SCREEN] Counter falling: "80,394 BTC → 313 BTC"

The following Monday, the Luna Foundation Guard says it has 313 bitcoin left. Out of 80,394.
An audit the foundation released later put the total spent defending the peg at about 2.8 billion dollars.

(beat)

[AI-02 variant] Same dark apartment, now dawn light, phone face-down on the table

Around 40 billion dollars of value, gone in a matter of days.
Some victims had put in their life savings. Many had trusted the word "stable."

## 8. The Lunatics (12:38–13:44)

[AI-12] A dark wall of hundreds of anonymous glowing chat bubbles and avatar placeholders, slowly dimming one by one, no readable names

Who were the Lunatics?
In Korea, they gathered in group chat rooms and online forums. Overseas, on Reddit, Discord and Twitter.
For two years, they shared memes, price targets, and a kind of belonging. Kwon was their captain.

Many of them weren't gamblers.
The stories posted online came from office workers, retirees, young couples saving for a home, people who had moved money out of a bank because Anchor paid many times more.

[SCREEN] Self-made forum mock-up (no real usernames): a pinned post labeled "Support resources"

After the crash, those same communities changed overnight. The memes stopped.
Moderators of the biggest English-language forum pinned links to support helplines at the top of the page.
In Korea, where LUNA had traded on the country's biggest exchanges, those exchanges moved to delist it.
Groups of Korean investors filed criminal complaints and lawsuits against Terra's founders.

(beat)

We are not naming any of them here. Their losses are not entertainment.
But every number in this story was somebody's rent, somebody's savings, somebody's plan.

## 9. The dominoes (13:44–15:09)

[AI-13] Giant glass dominoes shaped like skyscrapers toppling one into the next across a dark, wet plaza, red light reflecting

Terra did not fall alone.
Remember Three Arrows Capital, the Singapore-based hedge fund that helped lead that billion-dollar LUNA sale? It had made a large bet on LUNA. When LUNA went to zero, so did the bet.
By the start of July, a court in the British Virgin Islands had ordered the fund liquidated.

[SCREEN] Domino timeline: "Terra (May)" → "Three Arrows (June)" → "Voyager, Celsius (July)" → "FTX (November)"

The firms that had lent to it were next.
Voyager Digital, which said Three Arrows had defaulted on a loan of about 650 million dollars, filed for bankruptcy on July 5.
Celsius Network, a crypto lender that had promised its users high yields, froze withdrawals in June and filed for bankruptcy on July 13.

And then there was FTX.
At the criminal trial of FTX founder Sam Bankman-Fried, a former top executive of his trading firm, Alameda Research, testified that after the crash, lenders started demanding their money back in June 2022, and that Alameda repaid them using money that came from FTX customers.
FTX collapsed in November 2022. Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud a year later.

(beat)

Terra didn't commit FTX's fraud. A jury put that on Bankman-Fried.
But according to that testimony, the panic that began in May was part of what pushed customer money into the hole.

## 10. Terra 2.0 (15:09–15:56)

[AI-14] A new glowing coin rising out of grey ash in a dark room, a thin crack already running across its surface

While the dominoes fell, Kwon proposed a fix: start over.
On May 25, Terra's community voted for a new blockchain. On May 28, it went live.
The new coin was also called LUNA. The old chain was renamed Terra Classic, and the old coins became LUNA Classic and USTC.

Holders of the old coins received airdrops of new LUNA, based on what they held and when.
One thing was missing. There was no new UST. Terra 2.0 launched without an algorithmic stablecoin at all.
The new LUNA briefly traded above 17 dollars, then quickly fell as people sold what they'd been given.
For many old holders, it was nowhere near what they had lost.

## 11. The chase (15:56–17:06)

[SCREEN] Map animation: Seoul → Singapore → Serbia → Montenegro

When the collapse hit, Kwon was living in Singapore.
In September 2022, a Seoul court issued a warrant for his arrest, and Interpol issued a red notice.
He said he wasn't on the run. But nobody seemed to know where he was.
Days after the warrant, Singapore police said he was no longer in Singapore.
In October, Korea's foreign ministry invalidated his passport. Online, he kept posting as if nothing had changed.

[AI-08] Airport departure gate at night, a lone traveler's silhouette from behind, passport in hand

In March 2023, at the airport in Podgorica, the capital of Montenegro, police stopped a man trying to board a private flight.
He was carrying a forged Costa Rican passport.
It was Do Kwon.
A Montenegrin court sentenced him to four months in prison for the forged documents.

[SCREEN] Two flags, South Korea and the United States, pulling a single document in opposite directions

Both South Korea and the United States wanted him.
For more than a year and a half, Montenegro's courts and ministers went back and forth over which country should get him first.
In the end, he was extradited to the United States, on the last day of 2024.

## 12. The verdicts (17:06–18:27)

[AI-15] An empty wood-paneled courtroom at night, a single desk lamp on, a gavel resting on a stack of papers, slow push-in

America went after Kwon twice. Once in civil court. Once in criminal court.

[SCREEN] SEC document on screen, key lines highlighted

The SEC sued Terraform and Kwon in February 2023.
Remember Chai? The SEC alleged that Chai's payments were not actually processed on the Terra blockchain, and that transactions were copied onto the chain to make it look as if they were.
In April 2024, a jury in New York found Terraform and Kwon liable for fraud.
Afterwards, the SEC said the trial had exposed the defendants' lies about the use of the Terra blockchain to settle transactions.
In June 2024, Terraform and Kwon agreed to a settlement of about 4.47 billion dollars, and Terraform agreed to wind down.

[SCREEN] Plea quote on black, white text, slow fade-in

Then came the criminal case. In August 2025, Kwon pleaded guilty to two fraud charges. And in court, he said this:
"In 2021, I made false and misleading statements about why UST regained its peg."

(beat)

That was the hidden rescue. The question from earlier, answered by the man himself.

In December 2025, the judge sentenced him to 15 years in prison. That was longer than the 12 years prosecutors had asked for.
The judge called it a fraud on an "epic, generational scale."

## 13. The Korean side of the story (18:27–19:42)

[AI-02 variant] Rows of Seoul apartment windows at night, a few still lit

Most English coverage stops there. But the biggest group of retail victims was in Korea.
According to figures from Korean financial authorities, about 280,000 people in Korea held LUNA when it collapsed.
Within days of the crash, Seoul prosecutors revived a financial-crimes unit nicknamed the "Yeouido Grim Reaper," after the district where Korea's financial industry lives. Terra was one of its first cases.

[SCREEN] Korean newspaper-style headline graphic (self-made, no real mastheads), translated on screen

In April 2023, Korean prosecutors indicted Daniel Shin and several others.
Prosecutors allege that Chai made ordinary payments look as if they were running on Terra.
Shin denies wrongdoing. His lawyers say he left Terra two years before the collapse and had nothing to do with it.
Korean courts twice turned down requests to arrest him, and his trial is still going on.

[SCREEN] Map: New York prison icon → arrow → Seoul courthouse icon

And Do Kwon may not be done with Korean justice.
U.S. prosecutors said they would not oppose a transfer to Korea after he serves half his sentence.
In Korea, he would still face separate charges, including under the capital markets law. Some Korean media have reported that a conviction there could mean more than 30 years.

## 14. Why the dream keeps coming back (19:42–21:10)

[AI-16] A single glowing coin balanced on the tip of a spinning top, slowly wobbling over black water, teal and red reflections

Terra wasn't the first algorithmic stablecoin to break.
In June 2021, a project called Iron Finance saw its partly algorithmic stablecoin lose its peg, while its sister token fell from around 60 dollars to almost zero in less than a day.
Same design idea. Same spiral. Smaller scale.

So why does the idea keep coming back?
Because the dream is powerful. Money as steady as a dollar, without anyone needing to hold the dollars.
And because the mechanism works perfectly, right up until the moment people stop believing in it. Confidence is the collateral.

[SCREEN] Self-made math card: "14,000,000,000 UST × ~19.5% ≈ $2.7B a year in promised interest"

And the yield makes the math look like magic. Run the numbers yourself.
Fourteen billion dollars of deposits, at nearly 20 percent, means roughly 2.7 billion dollars a year in promised interest.
Anchor's borrowers never paid anything close to that. The difference had to come from somewhere: reserves, subsidies, and new money coming in.

[SCREEN] Simple card: "GENIUS Act (U.S., July 2025): payment stablecoins backed 1:1 by cash and short-term Treasuries"

Lawmakers noticed. In July 2025, the United States passed the GENIUS Act, which requires payment stablecoins to be backed one-for-one by reserves like cash and short-term Treasuries.

(beat)

This isn't investment advice. It's a pattern.
When a yield is far above what anything safe pays, the money is coming from somewhere. If you can't see where, it might be coming from you.

Terra didn't fail because nobody warned it would.
It failed because a 20 percent promise was more convincing than every warning.
