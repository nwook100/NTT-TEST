## 0. Cold open (0:00–0:35)

[SCREEN] Tokyo at night, late-1980s neon. A number climbs on screen: "~50% of the world's chips"

1990. Of the ten biggest chip companies on Earth, six were Japanese.
Number one: NEC. Number two: Toshiba. Hitachi and Fujitsu right behind them.
Japan made roughly half of all the chips sold in the world.
In America, people were saying Silicon Valley was finished.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Headline-style graphic: "2012 — Elpida files for bankruptcy"

Twenty-two years later, Japan's last major memory chipmaker filed for bankruptcy.
Its factories were sold to an American rival.
So how does the undisputed champion fall that fast?
It wasn't one mistake. It was a war fought on three fronts, and Japan lost on all three.
This is one of the most dramatic collapses in the history of technology.

[SCREEN] Channel logo sting (under 3 seconds)

## 1. How Japan got to the top (0:35–2:40)

[SCREEN] 1970s factory footage (stock) + caption "VLSI Project, 1976"

It starts in 1976.
Japan's government brings its biggest electronics companies into one room.
Companies that normally fought each other for every customer.
The goal was simple: build the chip technology to catch America. Together.
It was called the VLSI Project.

[SCREEN] Diagram: government in the center, arrows to five company logos, "shared research lab"

The companies shared a research lab, shared engineers, and shared what they learned.
Once the research was done, they went back to competing.
But now they were competing with world-class technology.

[SCREEN] Close-up of a DRAM chip + caption "DRAM = a computer's short-term memory"

Japan bet on one product: DRAM.
It's the memory a computer uses to hold data while it works. Every computer needs it, and it needs a lot of it.
That makes DRAM a volume game. Whoever makes it best and cheapest, at the biggest scale, wins.

And Japanese companies built it the way they built cars and TVs.
No defects. Built to last. Perfect.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Infographic: "HP quality test, 1980" — bar chart, US vs Japanese failure rates

In March 1980, an executive at Hewlett-Packard stood up at an industry conference and dropped a bombshell.
HP had tested hundreds of thousands of memory chips from American and Japanese suppliers.
The Japanese chips failed far less often.
Not a few percent better. Several times better.
The room went quiet. American chipmakers had always assumed they were the best in the world.

[SCREEN] Japan market-share line rising (1980 → 1986 → 1990)

Japan won on quality. Then it went after price.
Japanese companies had something American companies didn't: patient money.
They belonged to huge industrial groups with banks behind them.
They could keep building factories even when prices fell, and wait for their rivals to run out of cash.
By the mid-1980s, Japan had passed the United States in chips.

## 2. Intel walks away (2:40–3:35)

[SCREEN] Intel logo, 1970s photos (stock style) + caption "Intel 1103, 1970"

In 1970, a young company called Intel sold the world's first commercial DRAM chip.
Memory was Intel's identity. It was the business that built the company.

[SCREEN] Two silhouettes in an office, dim light. Caption "1985"

But by 1985, Intel was losing money on every memory chip it sold.
Intel's president Andy Grove later described a conversation with his CEO, Gordon Moore.
Grove asked: if we got kicked out, and the board brought in a new CEO, what would he do?
Moore answered without hesitating: he would get us out of memories.
Grove looked at him and said: then why don't we walk out the door, come back in, and do it ourselves?

[SCREEN] Intel logo + "EXIT DRAM" stamp effect

So they did. Intel walked away from DRAM.
The company that started the memory market had been pushed out of it.

(beat)

It turned out to be one of the best decisions in business history. Intel bet everything on microprocessors, the brains of the personal computer.
But in 1985, it looked like surrender.
Japan was on top of the world.

---

## 3. America strikes back (3:35–5:05)

[SCREEN] US Capitol, American flag. Music shifts to tension

Washington didn't see this as normal competition.
Chips go into missiles. Into fighter jets. Into satellites.
In 1987, a Pentagon advisory panel, the Defense Science Board, warned that America was becoming dangerously dependent on foreign chips.
Depending on another country for them? That was a national security problem.

[SCREEN] Title "Plaza Accord, 1985" + yen/dollar chart

The first blow was currency.
After the Plaza Accord in 1985, the yen surged against the dollar.
Japanese companies could sell the same chip for the same dollar price and take home far fewer yen.

[SCREEN] Document-style graphic "U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Agreement, 1986"

The second blow came in 1986: the U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Agreement.
Two key points.
Stop selling chips below cost.
And open Japan's own market to foreign chips, with an expected target of 20 percent.

[SCREEN] Caption "1987: 100% tariffs" + silhouettes of Japanese electronics

Then in 1987, President Reagan said Japan wasn't keeping its promises.
He slapped 100 percent tariffs on hundreds of millions of dollars' worth of Japanese electronics.

[SCREEN] Caption "SEMATECH, 1987" + map pin on Austin, Texas

And America copied Japan's own playbook.
In 1987, U.S. chipmakers and the government launched SEMATECH, a shared research consortium, to rebuild American manufacturing.
The student had become the teacher. Now the teacher was taking notes.

(beat)

At this point, it looks like a simple story. America used its power and crushed Japan.
But here's the twist.
The company that took Japan's crown wasn't American.

---

## 4. The real winner (5:05–6:45)

[SCREEN] Map zooming in on South Korea. Caption "February 1983 — Tokyo"

In February 1983, in a hotel in Tokyo, the founder of a Korean company made an announcement.
His company was best known for TVs and home appliances.
Now it was going into memory chips.
That company was Samsung.
Almost every expert expected it to fail.
It had less technology, less experience, and fewer engineers than the Japanese giants.

[SCREEN] Split screen: Japanese companies on one side, Korean company on the other

But the 1986 agreement changed the game.
It put a floor under Japanese chip prices. Anyone who could sell cheaper suddenly had an opening.
And America was watching Japan. Not Korea.

[SCREEN] 1990s desktop PCs (stock)

The market itself was changing too.
Japanese DRAM was designed for mainframes, the giant computers run by banks and governments.
Some of that memory came with a 25-year guarantee.

But in the 1990s, the biggest buyer of memory shifted from mainframes to the personal computer.
And PC makers didn't need memory that lasted 25 years.
They needed memory that lasted a few years, cost less, and showed up fast and in huge volumes.
Samsung decided a three-year guarantee was good enough.

(beat)

[SCREEN] Thumbnail text returns, full screen: "TOO PERFECT"

Japan's greatest weapon, perfect quality, had turned into a cost it couldn't escape.
Meanwhile, the newcomers sold memory that was good enough, for a lot less.

[SCREEN] DRAM price chart crashing, with "new fab" icons appearing at the bottom

And Samsung did something else that Japan had once done, and then stopped doing.
When chip prices crashed, it kept building factories.
In memory, the downturn is when the next winner is decided. The company with the newest factory when demand returns takes the market.

[SCREEN] Animated DRAM ranking (1990 → 1992)

In 1992, Samsung became the world's number one DRAM maker.
It held that position for more than thirty years, until 2025, when it lost the top spot by quarterly revenue to another Korean company: SK hynix.

---

## 5. The giants fall (6:45–7:55)

[SCREEN] Nikkei crash chart + caption "The Lost Decade"

For Japan, the timing could not have been worse.
In the early 1990s, its economic bubble burst. Stock and land prices collapsed, and the banks behind the big industrial groups were drowning in bad loans.
That patient money was gone.
Japan closed its wallet. Its rivals opened theirs.

[SCREEN] NEC + Hitachi logos merging → "Elpida, 1999"

In 1999, NEC and Hitachi merged their DRAM businesses into a new company: Elpida.
Later, Mitsubishi's DRAM business joined too.
Former rivals, now fighting side by side.
It was Japan's last big hope in memory.

[SCREEN] 2008 financial-crisis chart → yen chart hitting record highs → "2012: Elpida bankrupt"

Then came the 2008 financial crisis, and a brutal crash in memory prices.
Then the yen climbed to record highs, making every Japanese chip more expensive abroad.
In February 2012, Elpida filed for bankruptcy. The next year, it was bought by America's Micron.

(beat)

Japan's DRAM industry, once number one in the world, was gone.

---

## 6. Did Japan really lose? (7:55–8:40)

[SCREEN] Map of Japan; equipment and materials icons light up one by one

But if the story ends here, you've only seen half of it.
Japan lost the race to make memory chips. It never lost the race to make the tools and materials that chips are made with.
When Japan restricted exports of three key chemicals to South Korea in 2019, the whole industry held its breath. That's why.

[SCREEN] Title "Rapidus, 2022" + map marker on Kumamoto

And now Japan wants back in.
A government-backed startup called Rapidus is chasing the most advanced 2-nanometer chips, aiming for mass production in 2027.
And Taiwan's TSMC has opened a factory in Kumamoto, in southern Japan.

(beat)

[SCREEN] The opening Tokyo skyline returns, this time present day

It took Japan about twenty years to fall from the top.
How long will it take to climb back?
Or is that even possible?
